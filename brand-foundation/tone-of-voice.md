# Numena: Tone of Voice

We take complex human work seriously. But we don't need to sound complicated to prove it.

Our voice is thoughtful, clear and grounded. It respects the intelligence of psychologists without trying to impress them with jargon. It gives people room to think, while helping them see connections, question assumptions and make better-informed decisions.

We're not trying to sound like a medical institution, an AI company, or a productivity tool.

We sound human.

Use these principles as guidance rather than rigid rules. The voice can become warmer, more direct or more playful depending on the context. But it should always feel considered, honest and quietly confident.

## Voice principles

### 1. Be thoughtful

We believe good psychological work requires attention, interpretation and judgment. Our language should reflect that.

We don't rush to conclusions. We make space for complexity without making things unnecessarily complicated.

**Make room for nuance.** Avoid absolute claims when the evidence doesn't support them. Use language that reflects uncertainty honestly.

Instead of:

> Understand your clients better with AI.

Say:

> See patterns across your work, while keeping the context behind them in view.

**Choose precision over cleverness.** Use specific words that describe what actually happens.

Prefer:

> Trace a hypothesis back to the observations that informed it.

Over:

> Unlock deeper clinical insights.

The first tells you something. The second sounds like marketing.

**Let the reader think.** Don't explain every implication. Sometimes the strongest writing gives someone enough information to make the connection themselves.

Short sentences help.
So does a little silence.

### 2. Be human

Psychology is about people. Our language should never make their work feel like a data-processing problem.

We speak naturally, respectfully and directly. We don't sound clinical unless the context requires it.

**Speak one-to-one.** Use you, your, we and our. Write as though you're speaking with a thoughtful professional, not presenting to an audience.

> Your notes contain more than documentation. They contain the reasoning behind your work.

**Use natural language.** Contractions are fine. Starting with and, but or so is fine. Avoid corporate phrases like:

- leverage
- optimize workflows
- unlock insights
- drive efficiency
- empower practitioners
- next-generation
- AI-powered

If a simpler word works, use it.

**Respect the person behind the work.** Never reduce psychologists to users, clinicians to workflows, or clients to data.

Prefer:

> Keep the context behind each observation visible.

Over:

> Centralize client data for better decision-making.

The difference is small in words, but significant in attitude.

### 3. Be rigorous

We don't use confidence as a substitute for evidence.

Our voice should communicate competence without pretending to know more than we do. We explain difficult ideas clearly, distinguish observation from interpretation, and make uncertainty visible when it matters.

**Say what we know.** Be specific about what the product does and doesn't do.

> The system helps you organize observations, connect evidence and examine competing hypotheses.

Not:

> AI understands your clients and helps you make better diagnoses.

**Don't hide uncertainty.** Words like might, possible, uncertain and we don't know yet aren't weaknesses when they're accurate.

They are part of responsible reasoning.

> This pattern appears in three sessions. It's worth investigating, but it's not enough to establish an explanation.

That's more trustworthy than manufactured certainty.

**Explain the reasoning.** Whenever possible, show the relationship between an insight and the information behind it.

> See which observations support a pattern, which contradict it, and what information is still missing.

We don't just present conclusions. We make the path to them visible.

### 4. Be quietly confident

We know what we believe in. We don't need to shout about it.

The brand should feel assured rather than authoritative, intelligent rather than academic, and serious without becoming austere.

**Make a clear statement.** Don't hide behind vague marketing language.

> Good clinical reasoning isn't just about finding patterns. It's about knowing how much confidence to place in them.

That's stronger than:

> We're redefining the future of psychological practice.

**Avoid hype.** No grand claims about transforming healthcare, revolutionizing psychology or replacing professional judgment.

We don't need to promise the future. We can show what we're building.

> Less time reconstructing your reasoning. More time thinking about it.

**Use restraint.** Not every sentence needs emphasis. Avoid excessive exclamation marks, dramatic claims and superlatives.

The confidence should come from the idea itself. Quiet confidence is still confidence.

## A simple test

Before publishing something, ask:

- **Is it clear?** Could someone understand it without knowing our terminology?
- **Is it honest?** Are we claiming more than we can support?
- **Is it human?** Would a psychologist actually say this?
- **Is it useful?** Does the reader learn something, see something differently, or understand what the product does?
- **Is it ours?** Could any generic AI startup say the same thing?

If the answer to the last question is yes, rewrite it.
